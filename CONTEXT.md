# CONTEXT — Automated Droplet Size Measurement

This document explains **what** we are measuring and **why** each design choice was made.
The concrete build spec is in `INSTRUCTIONS.md`. Read this first so the choices in the
build spec make sense; do not "optimize away" decisions documented here without checking
the rationale.

---

## 1. The experiment

- Charged liquid droplets (ethanol) are generated by an inkjet dispenser and confined in a
  **Paul trap**. The process is recorded on video; the operator later saves individual
  video frames as still images of trapped drops.
- The camera images the trap **through the ~1 mm gap between the Paul-trap rods**, so only a
  bright horizontal band of the field of view is actually lit — the rest of the frame is dark.
- The drops are illuminated such that each in-focus drop shows **two bright spots** (specular
  reflections of the trapping laser, from the air/ethanol refractive-index interfaces). With a
  newer diffuse ring light the drops are more symmetric and ring-like, but the two bright spots
  are still visible.
- **Goal:** measure each drop's diameter in the **horizontal** and **vertical** directions,
  in microns, so drop size (and its shrinkage as the drop evaporates across successive frames)
  can be tracked.

## 2. Why calibration is needed

The measurement software reports sizes in **pixels**. To convert to microns, the operator
images a **calibration slide** — a 10×10 grid of **0.1 mm (100 µm) squares** — at the *same*
magnification/focus as the drops (only the slide is swapped in; optics are untouched). The
known 100 µm box spacing gives the pixel→micron scale.

- The calibration scale depends **only on optics** (magnification/focus), **not on exposure or
  lighting**. Exposure may change between sessions; the scale does not, as long as
  magnification/focus are untouched.
- **Working assumption:** every time in-focus, resolved drops are imaged, a calibration slide
  is imaged afterward at the same optics. Each session therefore has its own calibration.
  Calibration is **not** reused across days unless the operator explicitly says so.

## 3. What the real images actually look like (validated on sample files)

These properties were confirmed by inspecting real files during design. The pipeline must be
robust to them.

**Drop images** (`*_diffcalib_frame###.jpg`, native ≈ 980×1340):
- **Strongly banded, non-uniform background** — a bright horizontal stripe, a dark stripe
  through the middle where drops sit, brighter again below. A single global brightness
  threshold **fails** here (it locks onto the bright bands and misses the drops). Background
  must be flattened first.
- Drops are **faint and low-contrast**, **ring-shaped** (bright rim, darker center, plus the
  two bright laser spots), typically **~14–28 px** across.
- **1–3 drops per image** (2 is common), **not always centered**, well separated from each
  other. Some drops are blurry / out of focus and should be flagged for the operator to discard.
- Expected physical sizes: **~40–60 µm** typical, rarely above 60 µm; drops shrink over
  successive frames as they evaporate.
- Files are currently **JPEG** (lossy). PNG/TIFF is preferred going forward because JPEG
  block artifacts are worst exactly at these faint, low-contrast edges.

**Calibration images** (`*_cam_##.png`, native ≈ 1003×1357):
- Grid is **dark lines on a bright background**, but only visible **within the bright band**
  (the 1 mm rod gap); the rest of the frame is dark.
- The grid is **rotated by a few degrees** (the operator sometimes rotates the image by hand to
  draw straight lines; residual rotation remains regardless). The calibrator must **de-rotate**
  before measuring spacing.
- The slide surface has **many impurity specks**. These are **non-periodic**, so a
  spacing/periodicity-based method ignores them — this is a major reason we measure spacing,
  not individual features.

## 4. Measurement philosophy (the important decisions)

### 4a. Measure grid *spacing*, not hand-counted boxes
The operator's previous method (measure 10 boxes and 1 box, average the two ratios) produced a
~3% internal disagreement (e.g. 2.5 vs 2.42 µm/px). Instead we fit **all gridlines at once**:
project the de-rotated grid to 1-D per axis, find the line positions, and take the
**least-squares slope** of position-vs-index. This uses every line, returns a fit residual as a
built-in quality check, and is immune to the impurity specks. **X and Y scales are kept
separate** (they may differ slightly; a large difference is a useful warning of non-square
pixels or distortion).

*Validation result:* on the sample grid this gave **41.30 px/100µm (X, 2.42 µm/px)** and
**40.86 px/100µm (Y, 2.45 µm/px)**, fit residuals ~0.27 px, both within ~1% of the operator's
hand-derived **2.46 µm/px**, and the two axes agree to ~1%. The automated method is *more*
self-consistent than the manual one.

### 4b. Drop edge = where signal separates from background, on a *flattened* image
The operator's manual method draws a line across the drop and reads the intensity profile,
calling the drop edge where intensity leaves/returns to "background." We reproduce that idea but
**2-D and automated**: flatten the banded background (morphological top-hat), estimate the
background level and noise σ, threshold at **background + k·σ**, fill the ring interior, and
measure the blob's horizontal and vertical extent (with sub-pixel edge interpolation), plus an
ellipse fit as a cross-check.

- The single tuning knob is **`k`** (how many σ above background counts as "drop"). It is set
  once so a clear drop reproduces the operator's trusted horizontal number, then held fixed.
- The half-maximum criterion is **wrong** for this geometry — the two bright spots create a
  central dip, so a half-max cut would split one drop into two. Background-departure is correct.

### 4c. Known caveat: vertical definition differs from the operator's old hand numbers
Validation showed the operator's **horizontal** hand-measurements match the automation closely
(easy to judge — the spots are much brighter than background, steep edges). The operator's
**vertical** hand-measurements ran **larger** than the automation (e.g. 22 and 28 px vs ~13–18
px). Cause: vertical contrast is low, so by eye the operator included the shallow *shoulders* of
the profile — only a few counts above background, down in the noise (σ≈10). The automation cuts
at the true separation from background and hugs the visible drop.

**Consequence to respect:** automated vertical diameters read **systematically smaller** than
historical hand-measured verticals. Within an all-automated dataset everything is
self-consistent. **Do not plot old-manual-V and new-auto-V on the same curve** — there is a
definitional offset. (Re-running old images through the pipeline makes them comparable.)
Horizontal and calibration have no such offset.

### 4d. Multi-drop handling
With the current lighting each drop is a single compact blob, and drops are well separated, so
connected-components labeling on the flattened+thresholded image gives one component per drop.
A small morphological close bridges the two-spot/ring structure into one blob. Frame-edge
artifacts (bright bands touching the image border) must be rejected by position/shape. If two
real drops ever sit closer than the intra-drop gap, no automatic method can split them reliably
— the per-drop QC overlay is the backstop (the operator sees the error and discards it).

### 4e. QC overlay for every drop
Every drop image gets a saved overlay (original with the measured box/ellipse drawn on) so the
operator can eyeball each measurement and discard dust specks, blurry drops, or merge/split
errors. For a scientific measurement this visual audit trail is required, not optional.

## 5. Operating constraints baked into the design

- **Exposure/lighting** must be consistent **within any set of drops that will be compared or
  pooled** (e.g. an evaporation series). It may change between sessions; the σ-relative `k`
  self-adapts to modest changes, but large changes warrant a glance at a clear drop's QC overlay.
- **Never saturate the drops** (pixels clipping at 255). Once clipped, the true edge is lost and
  no threshold recovers it. Saturated drops are flagged in the output.
- **Prefer PNG/TIFF** over JPEG going forward.

## 6. Non-goals
- Not tracking drops across frames or fitting evaporation curves (measurement only; the CSV
  feeds any downstream analysis).
- Not measuring absolute physical drop volume or 3-D shape — only projected H and V diameters.
- Not correcting the possibility that the bright-footprint edge differs from the drop's true
  physical edge by a few µm; the operator accepts a few-µm accuracy and prioritizes
  **consistency/repeatability**, which the automation delivers.
